For parents

Phones, children and family: practical tips

The app helps enforce rules, but good rules are made by families. This page covers how much screen time suits different ages, how to talk with your child and when you should be concerned.

How much screen time suits each age

Screen time is time your child spends looking at a phone, tablet or television for entertainment. Schoolwork and video calls with relatives don't count.

Under 2

Not recommendedexcept for video calls with loved ones

  • A child's brain develops through conversation and play with people.
  • Don't use a phone to calm your child.
Ages 2–5

Up to 1 houra day; less is better

  • Choose high-quality educational apps.
  • Watch together and talk about what you see.
Ages 6–12

Consistent limitsour suggestion: 1–2 hours for games and videos

  • Screens shouldn't interfere with sleep, schoolwork or outdoor play.
  • No phone for one hour before bed.
Ages 13–17

Rules made togethermore freedom, more responsibility

  • Discuss the rules with your teenager instead of giving orders.
  • Focus on sleep and schoolwork, not every minute.

Sources: recommendations from the World Health Organization (2019) and the American Academy of Pediatrics. “1–2 hours” for ages 6–12 is NIGOH Family's own suggestion, not a medical guideline.

8 tips for a family that gets along with phones

Talk before installing

Explain that the app is there for your child's safety, not to spy on them. Show them that they can see every rule under “My rules”.

Write the rules together

Make a one-page “family agreement”: when and how long phones can be used, where they aren't allowed and what happens if a rule is broken. Put it somewhere everyone can see it.

Set an example

If parents look at their phones during meals, children will do the same. “No phone” rules should apply to everyone.

Phone-free places and times

At the table, in the bedroom after lights out, and during lessons. Charge the phone outside the bedroom at night.

Reward, don't just punish

Give extra time for good schoolwork or helping at home. Constantly taking the phone away as punishment teaches children to hide things.

Take requests seriously

When your child asks for extra time, answer them — even if the answer is “no”. If requests go unanswered, they will look for another way.

Give more freedom with age

Review the rules every year. A teenager who acts responsibly should earn more trust.

Talk about risks

Strangers online, sending photos, scams and dangerous “games”. Say: “If anything scares you, tell me — I won't punish you.”

How to talk

One sentence can open up a conversation or shut it down.

Try saying

  • Let's work out together how much gaming time is enough.
  • I want to know that you're safe.
  • You can ask for extra time — I'll answer you.
  • Show me this game. How do you play it?

Try not to say

  • I can see everything you do.
  • You're glued to your phone again!
  • If you don't listen, I'll break your phone.
  • All games are bad for you.

When to be concerned

If several signs appear together and continue for a long time, talk with your child and, if necessary, consult the school psychologist or a doctor.

  • Goes to bed late and is sleepy in the morning
  • Gets angry when the phone is taken away
  • School grades suddenly fall
  • Hides the phone or changes the password
  • Withdraws from friends and outdoor play
  • Complains of headaches and tired eyes
